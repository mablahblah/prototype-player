What I Learned

A/B testing was definitely discussed at the beginning of the project. There was a risk of not being able to attribute success directly to features, and worse, we wouldn't know if a feature was hurting engagement.

The data and feedback* collected and synthesized to build this case study support this:

  1. 01

    Pre-written example questions hurt engagement

    Out of students who typed their own first question, ~66% asked a second question. Out of students who tapped a pre-written prompt, only ~53% asked a second.

  2. 02

    Students found Pro-Tips distracting

    "...RE students found pro tips distracting, didn't care for them." - Student Interview, September 2025.

We sacrificed this clarity for speed. It was a good reminder of the "Good, Fast, Cheap" rule. You can only pick 2.

*Note: this data and feedback were collected after this project was complete.

Virtual Instructor

August 2023 - April 2024

Started with a prototype, and rapidly iterated with a team over 2 quarters, focusing on improving engagement. Engagement rose from 3% to 16.8%, beating our goal of 15%, while maintaining a high answer quality from the AI.

Context

Aceable is an edtech company that specializes in courses with required hours in regulated spaces, focusing primarily on driver's ed and real-estate licensing. In a survey of students who abandoned their courses without completing, 38% wanted access to an instructor they could check in with.

In May of 2023, the company held a hackathon, a week where any team could come up with any idea that could "benefit Aceable students, our teammates, or the company." One team created a prototype of a chat experience that used our LMS and ChatGPT to allow students to ask open-ended questions while taking the course.

Project Goal

My team was tasked with taking this prototype, which we called "Virtual Instructor," and validating further investment and deeper integration by testing whether students would open it, try it, and trust it in the moments when it mattered. The team agreed that repeat engagement would be the earliest good signal that Virtual Instructor was helping students.

Key Metrics

15%

of Real Estate Pre-License students ask two or more questions

while maintaining at least a

70%

answer accuracy.

Team Strategy

Because we had an existing prototype and a goal to work toward, the team decided to work iteratively. We met as a group twice a week to strategize, ideate, and prioritize our own individual work and the larger team's work. I pushed for us to focus, first, on some foundational work before we switched to prioritizing the projects we felt would have the greatest impact on engagement.

Foundational Work

There were 2 things that I felt needed to be addressed first.

01

Design Gap

Chat experiences are ubiquitous, but our prototype's experience defied the norms. If we fixed these things up front, we wouldn't have to fight them deeper into the project. Also, the experience itself, if unfamiliar, could hurt engagement.

Validating some assumptions through design research

"Chat experiences are ubiquitous" is an assumption I wanted to validate, despite a high confidence. I don't really use them very often. I gathered some examples from popular "texting" apps (as opposed to chatbot or collaboration/communication apps like Slack or Discord).

02

Automating Feedback Collection

This was built using ChatGPT's API. It was untested, and we were not certain it would be consistent and actually helpful to students. We had our metrics, but I wanted a stream of actual feedback from students. Interviews moved too slowly for something that would be changing constantly, so I worked on adding a way for feedback to be asked for and collected automatically.

I prototyped 3 options to discuss with the team, each with different trade-offs:

In-Chat Feedback Collection

Virtual Instructor can ask for feedback directly.

In-Chat Feedback Prototype

Image
A student marking a Virtual Instructor answer unhelpful, with a list of reasons to choose from.

  • High effort

  • Students provide feedback directly within the experience.

  • High potential response rate

In-Chat Links to Surveys

Virtual Instructor itself asks students to give feedback and provides a link to an external survey.

In-Chat Link to Survey Prototype

Image
A card over the Virtual Instructor chat asking the student to say more, with Not Right Now and Sure buttons.

  • Medium effort

  • Providing feedback takes students out of the experience.

  • High potential response rate

Emailed Surveys

Triggering an email survey asking students for feedback on Virtual Instructor after first usage.

Emailed Survey Prototype

Image
A notification at the top of the phone inviting the student to share their Virtual Instructor experience.

  • Low effort

  • Providing feedback would be completely detached from the experience.

  • Low potential response rate

The team agreed on option 1. Students leaving the experience would hurt engagement.

2 phases of feedback

There was actually a mini trade-off when we chose the "in-chat" feedback option. Open-ended feedback itself added a lot of friction, and would also reduce response rate. My prototype accounted for this with 2 stages of giving feedback, both optional. I wanted to build a user flow so it was clear to the team and our developers how the user could move through the experience.

Two-phase feedback user flow: Phase 1 collects coded thumbs-up/down feedback, Phase 2 collects optional open-ended feedback; each phase saves to the database and branches on success or failure.

Impact

I believe adding this feedback mechanism was wise, even though nothing emerged that gave us pause or required us to alter the work we were doing.

Building Engagement

Early on our team and stakeholders defined repeat engagement simply as "a student asks 2 or more questions." I saw it as a funnel:

  1. 01
    If we can get students to open Virtual Instructor, then they can try it.
  2. 02
    If they try Virtual Instructor and it works well, they'll continue using it when they struggle.
  3. 03
    If they continue using Virtual Instructor, then that indicates trust.
01

Open Virtual Instructor

Get as many people to open the experience as possible.

Onboarding Cards

The first release relied entirely on students seeing a new thing in their course and deciding to click it. There was nothing informing them what it was or what it could do for them. The button was redesigned to be more intuitive, but there was still no proper introduction to the feature.

Extending the onboarding card from our design system

Our design system and platform had a feature already that allowed us to contextually inform existing students that something in the platform changed. It was only meant to keep students from being disrupted by major changes or platform differences. This could be used to tell existing students about Virtual Instructor, but we extended the component and its functionality so it worked more like contextual onboarding. Students who got to a certain point in their course would be introduced to Virtual Instructor.

We added a "Try it Now" button that opened Virtual Instructor to reduce the friction toward a first-time use.

Pop-Up Answer Remediation

The tricky thing about measuring engagement on a tool that was meant to help students who might be struggling is that no two students struggle in the same way, or at the same moments. If we can't connect students to Virtual Instructor when it's of the most benefit, then it's less likely that the student will try it, especially if they're hyper-focused on making progress in their free time.

Just-in-time support

Virtual Instructor has the most value to students when they are struggling, but the initial chat experience was passive. We experimented with introducing it at a real moment of need, when students got a question wrong. They were always given an explanation, but could open Virtual Instructor and ask follow-up questions.

Taking the opportunity to fix up some crusty ol' components

The "remediation" UI that was in our design system was not accessible. I wanted to take the opportunity to update it so that the design would work whether or not Virtual Instructor was on or off, as well as making it accessible. This was entirely a re-skin. 02

Try Virtual Instructor

Get as many people as possible to try Virtual Instructor.

Example Questions

We sought an "A-ha!" moment with our students. At this time, customer service chatbots were more prevalent than AI-powered chat experiences, with answer trees and "talk to a human" limits. We wanted to differentiate Virtual Instructor by showing students the types of questions that Virtual Instructor COULD answer. If a student tried it themselves, we felt the resulting answer could be that "A-ha!" moment. "I can ask this ANYTHING!?"

Prototype

Everything we needed for this idea was already in our design system, so I went pretty quickly into prototyping the interaction to get a feel for it. The idea was that Virtual Instructor would be essentially introducing itself.

Virtual Instructor Intro & Example Question Prototype

Image
The Virtual Instructor chat introducing itself and listing example questions a student can ask.

Thinking about first-time users vs. ongoing users

The "first-time user" experience of the chat loading the example questions was long, and once a student had asked a question, it had no real value. I thought through the various cases and built a user flow to make sure I was covering my bases and to help explain to the development team the states they would have to think about.

User flow for the example-question experience, mapping the first-time and returning-user paths and the states the chat moves through.
03

Trust Virtual Instructor

Get as many people as possible to continue using Virtual Instructor.

First-Time Experience & Pro-Tips

Observing Virtual Instructor's answer quality over time, along with the student feedback we were receiving, we found some issues that were difficult and expensive to solve. The whole point of this project was to prove Virtual Instructor was worth this kind of further investment.

  1. 01

    Virtual Instructor had no memory

    This was a real tension on the team. AI was rapidly advancing and students' expectations of what it could do were outpacing what ours could do. Virtual Instructor had no context window at all. The value vs. cost of adding this in was a recurring conversation on the team.

  2. 02

    Virtual Instructor was inconsistent with Math

    We used ChatGPT under the hood, which was not great at math, especially the specialized math formulas real estate students were learning.

The best we could do without that investment was to explain the capabilities of Virtual Instructor to students up front. We felt taking accountability and explaining to students how to use Virtual Instructor effectively could build that trust in lieu of those improvements we hoped to make down the road.

We had already built a kind of "Virtual Instructor-guided intro" with the example question work. We added onto that an outro. After a student asked a question (or example question), and received an answer, then Virtual Instructor would send 1 additional message about what it could and couldn't do well. From then on, the student would get the default experience.

Except, when they loaded Virtual Instructor going forward, there would be a rotating "Pro-Tip." This would refresh students' memories if they hadn't used it in a while. It was intended to be unobtrusive, like a video game loading screen.
